Sequences · Part 1

Three ways to describe a sequence

Listing, closed form, and recurrence — plus the two most common patterns, arithmetic and geometric.

📝 View the quick-reference notes sheet for this topic →

What is a sequence?

A sequence is simply an ordered list of numbers, one for each positive integer index: \(a_1, a_2, a_3, \ldots\). There are three common ways to specify exactly what a sequence is.

1. Listing terms: write out the first few and assume the pattern continues (risky — see below).

2. Closed form: a direct formula for \(a_n\) in terms of \(n\), letting you jump straight to any term.

3. Recurrence: a rule defining \(a_n\) using the previous term \(a_{n-1}\), plus a starting value.
Worked example — all three side by side

Listing: \(4, 7, 10, 13, \ldots\) (add 3 each time, assumed pattern)

Closed form: \(a_n = 3n+1\) → instantly gives \(a_1=4\), \(a_{10}=31\), no earlier terms needed.

Recurrence: \(a_1=4\), \(a_n=a_{n-1}+3\) → each term needs the one before it computed first.

Listing terms alone is ambiguous — \(2,4,6,8\) could continue as \(10,12,\ldots\) (arithmetic), or could follow a completely different rule. Prefer a closed form or recurrence whenever precision actually matters.

Arithmetic sequences: constant difference

An arithmetic sequence adds the same fixed amount, called the common difference \(d\), each time. Its closed form is:

\[x_n = a+(n-1)d\]

Notice it's \((n-1)\), not \(n\) — because the first term \(x_1\) shouldn't have \(d\) added at all: \(x_1=a+(0)d=a\), exactly the starting value.
Worked example
\(a=5\), \(d=3\): the sequence runs \(5,8,11,14,\ldots\), and \[x_{10} = 5+(9)(3) = 32\]

Geometric sequences: constant ratio

A geometric sequence multiplies by the same fixed amount, the common ratio \(r\), each time. Its closed form is:

\[x_n = ar^{n-1}\]

Worked example
\(a=3\), \(r=2\): the sequence runs \(3,6,12,24,\ldots\), and \[x_6 = 3(2)^5 = 3(32)=96\]

Going from listed terms to a closed form

Given the first few terms, spot which type you have by checking whether consecutive terms have a constant difference (arithmetic) or a constant ratio (geometric) — always checking more than one pair, since one match could be a coincidence.

Try it: Given the sequence \(4, 12, 36, 108, \ldots\), find its type, its recurrence, and its closed form.
Show answer
Checking ratios: \(12/4=3\), \(36/12=3\), \(108/36=3\) — consistent, so this is geometric with \(a=4\), \(r=3\).

Recurrence: \(x_1=4\), \(x_n=3x_{n-1}\).

Closed form: \(x_n = 4(3)^{n-1}\). Check \(x_4\): \(4(3)^3=4(27)=108\) ✓
Try it: Find \(x_7\) for the arithmetic sequence with \(a=10\), \(d=-4\).
Show answer
\[x_7 = 10+(6)(-4) = 10-24=-14\]
