Undoing a matrix: determinants and inverses

A single number that tells you a lot about a matrix, and how to "divide" by one.

📝 View the quick-reference notes sheet for this topic →

The determinant: one number that summarises a matrix

For a \(2\times2\) matrix \(A=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}\), the determinant, written \(\det(A)\) or \(|A|\), is defined as:

\[\det(A) = ad-bc\]

That's it — multiply the main diagonal (\(a\) and \(d\)), multiply the other diagonal (\(b\) and \(c\)), and subtract.

Worked example
For \(A=\begin{pmatrix}3&2\\1&4\end{pmatrix}\): \[\det(A) = (3)(4)-(2)(1) = 12-2=10\]
The determinant tells you whether a matrix has an inverse at all. If \(\det(A)=0\), the matrix is called singular and has no inverse — just like you can't divide by zero with ordinary numbers.

Finding the inverse of a 2×2 matrix

The inverse of \(A\), written \(A^{-1}\), is the matrix that "undoes" \(A\) — multiplying \(A\) by \(A^{-1}\) (in either order) gives the identity matrix \(I\). For a \(2\times2\) matrix, there's a direct formula:

\[A^{-1} = \frac{1}{\det(A)}\begin{pmatrix} d & -b \\ -c & a \end{pmatrix}\]

In words: swap the two diagonal entries (\(a\) and \(d\)), flip the sign of the other two entries (\(b\) and \(c\)), then divide everything by the determinant.
Worked example
Find \(A^{-1}\) for \(A=\begin{pmatrix}3&2\\1&4\end{pmatrix}\) (using \(\det(A)=10\) from above): \[A^{-1} = \frac{1}{10}\begin{pmatrix} 4 & -2 \\ -1 & 3 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0.4 & -0.2 \\ -0.1 & 0.3 \end{pmatrix}\]
Only swap the diagonal entries (\(a\) and \(d\)) — the off-diagonal entries (\(b\) and \(c\)) just get their sign flipped, they don't swap position with each other.

Try it yourself

Try it: Is \(\begin{pmatrix}2&4\\1&2\end{pmatrix}\) invertible?
Show answer
\[\det = (2)(2)-(4)(1) = 4-4=0\] Since the determinant is 0, this matrix is singular — it has no inverse.
Try it: Find the inverse of \(\begin{pmatrix}5&3\\2&1\end{pmatrix}\).
Show answer
\[\det = (5)(1)-(3)(2) = 5-6=-1\] \[A^{-1} = \frac{1}{-1}\begin{pmatrix}1&-3\\-2&5\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}-1&3\\2&-5\end{pmatrix}\]
