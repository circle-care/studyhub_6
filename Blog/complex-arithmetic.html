Part 1 of 6: Arithmetic & the imaginary unit

Complex Numbers · Part 1

What actually IS a complex number?

Starting from scratch: why \(i\) exists, and how to add, subtract, multiply and divide with it.

📝 View the quick-reference notes sheet for this topic →

The problem that started it all

Take the equation \(x^2 + 1 = 0\). Rearranged, that's \(x^2 = -1\). If you only know about real numbers, this has no solution — squaring any real number (positive, negative, or zero) always gives you something that's zero or positive. You can never square your way down to \(-1\).

For centuries mathematicians just called equations like this "unsolvable" and moved on. But it turns out that if you're willing to invent a new kind of number — one whose square is genuinely negative — a huge amount of mathematics opens up, and a lot of previously "impossible" problems become solvable. That new number is called \(i\), the imaginary unit.

Definition: \(i\) is defined by the property \(i^2 = -1\). It isn't found on the real number line anywhere — it's a genuinely new kind of quantity.

Once you have \(i\), you can build an entire new family of numbers by combining it with the real numbers you already know.

Building a complex number

A complex number is anything of the form

\[z = a + bi\]

where \(a\) and \(b\) are ordinary real numbers. \(a\) is called the real part, written \(\text{Re}(z)\), and \(b\) is called the imaginary part, written \(\text{Im}(z)\).

A common point of confusion: \(\text{Im}(z) = b\), not \(bi\). The imaginary part is itself a plain real number — it's just the coefficient sitting in front of \(i\).

Every real number is secretly a complex number too — just one where \(b = 0\). So complex numbers aren't replacing the real numbers, they're an extension that contains them.

Adding and subtracting

This part is refreshingly simple: treat \(i\) like a variable you can't simplify further (a bit like \(x\) in algebra), and combine "like terms" — real parts with real parts, imaginary parts with imaginary parts.

Worked example
Add \((3+2i)\) and \((1-5i)\): \[(3+2i) + (1-5i) = (3+1) + (2-5)i = 4 - 3i\] Subtract them instead: \[(3+2i) - (1-5i) = (3-1) + (2-(-5))i = 2 + 7i\]
Try it: Compute \((5-3i) + (-2+7i)\).
Show answer
\[(5-3i) + (-2+7i) = (5-2) + (-3+7)i = 3 + 4i\]

Multiplying complex numbers

Multiplication works exactly like expanding two brackets in ordinary algebra (FOIL, if you were taught that name) — the only extra step is remembering to replace \(i^2\) with \(-1\) once it shows up.

Worked example
Multiply \((2+3i)(1-4i)\): \[(2+3i)(1-4i) = 2(1) + 2(-4i) + 3i(1) + 3i(-4i)\] \[= 2 - 8i + 3i - 12i^2\] Now substitute \(i^2 = -1\): \[= 2 - 5i - 12(-1) = 2 - 5i + 12 = 14 - 5i\]
Try it: Compute \((1+i)(1-i)\). (Notice anything interesting about the result?)
Show answer
\[(1+i)(1-i) = 1 - i + i - i^2 = 1 - (-1) = 2\] The imaginary terms cancelled out completely, leaving a plain real number. This is not a coincidence — it's an example of multiplying a number by its conjugate, covered next.

The complex conjugate — and why it matters

Every complex number \(z = a+bi\) has a partner called its conjugate, written \(\bar{z}\), formed just by flipping the sign of the imaginary part:

\[\bar{z} = a - bi\]

Conjugates are useful because multiplying a number by its own conjugate always destroys the imaginary part and leaves something purely real:

\[z\bar{z} = (a+bi)(a-bi) = a^2 - (bi)^2 = a^2 - b^2i^2 = a^2+b^2\] Since \(a^2+b^2\) is always a real, non-negative number, this trick is extremely useful — especially for division.

Dividing complex numbers

You can't "just divide" the way you can with real numbers, because having \(i\) in the denominator isn't considered a valid final answer. The fix: multiply top and bottom by the conjugate of the denominator. This turns the denominator into a real number, and then it's just ordinary division.

Worked example
Simplify \(\dfrac{1+i}{2-i}\):

The conjugate of the denominator \(2-i\) is \(2+i\). Multiply top and bottom by it:

\[\frac{1+i}{2-i} \times \frac{2+i}{2+i} = \frac{(1+i)(2+i)}{(2-i)(2+i)}\]

Expand the top:

\[(1+i)(2+i) = 2 + i + 2i + i^2 = 2+3i-1 = 1+3i\]

Expand the bottom (using the conjugate shortcut from above):

\[(2-i)(2+i) = 2^2+1^2 = 5\]

So:

\[\frac{1+i}{2-i} = \frac{1+3i}{5} = \frac{1}{5}+\frac{3}{5}i\]
Try it: Simplify \(\dfrac{3}{1+i}\).
Show answer
Multiply top and bottom by \(1-i\): \[\frac{3}{1+i} \times \frac{1-i}{1-i} = \frac{3(1-i)}{1^2+1^2} = \frac{3-3i}{2} = \frac{3}{2}-\frac{3}{2}i\]

A useful shortcut: powers of \(i\)

Since \(i^2=-1\), you can work out any higher power of \(i\) just by repeatedly multiplying by \(i\):

\[i^1=i,\quad i^2=-1,\quad i^3=i^2 \cdot i=-i,\quad i^4=i^2\cdot i^2=1\]

And then it repeats: \(i^5=i\), \(i^6=-1\), and so on, forever, in a cycle of length 4. So to find \(i^n\) for any large \(n\), you don't need to multiply it out — just find the remainder when \(n\) is divided by 4.

Try it: What is \(i^{27}\)?
Show answer
\(27 \div 4 = 6\) remainder \(3\), so \(i^{27} = i^3 = -i\).
