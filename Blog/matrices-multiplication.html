Matrices · Part 2

Multiplying matrices — not what you'd guess

Why you can't just multiply entry-by-entry, and the "row times column" method that actually works.

📝 View the quick-reference notes sheet for this topic →

Why multiplication isn't entry-by-entry

Given how addition worked (matching entries combined position by position), you might expect multiplication to work the same way. It doesn't — matrix multiplication is defined completely differently, using a "row times column" process, because matrices are designed to represent things like linear transformations and systems of equations, where this definition turns out to be the useful one.

The compatibility rule

Before you can multiply two matrices \(A\) and \(B\) at all, their dimensions have to match up in a specific way:

\(A\) is \(m\times n\) and \(B\) is \(n\times p\) — the number of columns in \(A\) must equal the number of rows in \(B\). If they match, the result \(AB\) is an \(m\times p\) matrix.

A handy way to check: write the dimensions side by side, e.g. \((2\times3)(3\times4)\). The two middle numbers (3 and 3) must match — if they do, they "cancel", leaving the outer numbers (2 and 4) as the answer's dimensions.

How to actually multiply: row times column

Each entry in the result comes from taking a whole row of \(A\) and a whole column of \(B\), multiplying matching positions together, then adding those products up.

Worked example
\[\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 5 & 6 \\ 7 & 8 \end{pmatrix}\]

Top-left entry of the answer: row 1 of the first matrix, column 1 of the second:

\[(1)(5)+(2)(7) = 5+14=19\]

Top-right: row 1, column 2:

\[(1)(6)+(2)(8) = 6+16=22\]

Bottom-left: row 2, column 1:

\[(3)(5)+(4)(7) = 15+28=43\]

Bottom-right: row 2, column 2:

\[(3)(6)+(4)(8) = 18+32=50\]

So the final answer is:

\[\begin{pmatrix} 19 & 22 \\ 43 & 50 \end{pmatrix}\]

The order matters: AB ≠ BA

Unlike ordinary number multiplication, matrix multiplication is not commutative — swapping the order can give a completely different answer, or might not even be defined at all (if the dimensions don't line up the other way round).

Always keep track of the order you're multiplying in. \(AB\) and \(BA\) are generally two different matrices — never assume they're equal, even if both are technically valid to compute.
Try it: Can you multiply a \(2\times3\) matrix by a \(2\times3\) matrix directly (in that order)? Why or why not?
Show answer
No — the compatibility rule requires the first matrix's number of columns (3) to match the second matrix's number of rows (2). Since \(3\neq2\), this multiplication isn't defined in that order.
Try it: Multiply \(\begin{pmatrix}2&0\\1&3\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&4\\2&-1\end{pmatrix}\).
Show answer
Top-left: \((2)(1)+(0)(2)=2\). Top-right: \((2)(4)+(0)(-1)=8\). Bottom-left: \((1)(1)+(3)(2)=7\). Bottom-right: \((1)(4)+(3)(-1)=1\). \[\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 8 \\ 7 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\]
