Part 6 of 6: Polynomials & the Fundamental Theorem of Algebra

Complex Numbers · Part 6

Why every polynomial equation has a solution

The payoff for inventing \(i\) in the first place — and maths' most famous equation.

📝 View the quick-reference notes sheet for this topic →

Solving quadratics that used to have "no solution"

Back in Part 1, you saw that \(x^2+1=0\) has no real solution — but does have complex solutions, \(x=\pm i\). The same idea extends to any quadratic. Take \(z^2+2z+5=0\). Using the quadratic formula:

\[z = \frac{-2\pm\sqrt{4-20}}{2} = \frac{-2\pm\sqrt{-16}}{2}\]

Previously you'd have stopped here and said "no real solutions" because of the negative number under the square root. But now, \(\sqrt{-16} = \sqrt{16}\cdot\sqrt{-1} = 4i\), so:

\[z = \frac{-2\pm4i}{2} = -1\pm2i\]

Whenever a quadratic's discriminant \(b^2-4ac\) is negative, the two roots are always a complex conjugate pair — they come out as \(p+qi\) and \(p-qi\) for some real \(p,q\). This is guaranteed, never a coincidence.

The Fundamental Theorem of Algebra

This pattern generalises dramatically. The Fundamental Theorem of Algebra states:

Every polynomial equation of degree \(n\geq1\) has at least one complex solution — and in fact, it can always be factorised into exactly \(n\) linear factors (counting repeats).

In other words, once you allow complex numbers, polynomial equations never "run out" of solutions the way they can with real numbers only. This is really the whole point of inventing \(i\): it doesn't just patch up quadratics, it completes the entire number system so that every polynomial factorises fully.

The Conjugate Root Theorem

There's a useful shortcut when a polynomial's coefficients are all real numbers (as they usually are in practice): complex roots always show up in conjugate pairs. If \(p+qi\) is a root, then \(p-qi\) is guaranteed to also be a root.

Worked example
Factorise \(z^3-z^2+4z-4\), given that \(z=1\) is one root.

Since \(z=1\) is a root, \((z-1)\) must be a factor. Dividing (by polynomial long division or synthetic division) gives:

\[z^3-z^2+4z-4 = (z-1)(z^2+4)\]

Now solve the remaining quadratic factor: \(z^2+4=0 \implies z^2=-4 \implies z=\pm2i\).

So the full factorisation over the complex numbers is:

\[z^3-z^2+4z-4 = (z-1)(z-2i)(z+2i)\]

Notice \(2i\) and \(-2i\) are a conjugate pair, exactly as the theorem predicts.

Practical tip: once you've found one real root of a cubic (often by trial with small integers like \(\pm1,\pm2\)), use polynomial division to reduce it to a quadratic — which you can then solve directly, complex roots and all.

Euler's identity — tying it all together

Recall Euler's formula from Part 4: \(e^{i\theta}=\cos\theta+i\sin\theta\). Substitute \(\theta=\pi\):

\[e^{i\pi} = \cos\pi + i\sin\pi = -1 + 0i = -1\]

Rearranged, this gives what's often called the most beautiful equation in mathematics:

\(e^{i\pi}+1=0\)

In a single line, it connects five of the most fundamental constants in maths — \(0\), \(1\), \(i\), \(e\), and \(\pi\) — each arising from a completely different branch of the subject (arithmetic, number theory, complex numbers, calculus, and geometry respectively). It's a fitting place to end the unit: everything you've built up, from "what if \(i^2=-1\)" to full polynomial factorisation, comes together in this one equation.

Try it yourself
