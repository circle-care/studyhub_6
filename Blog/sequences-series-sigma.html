From sequences to series: adding the terms up

What a series actually is, and the compact sigma notation used to write sums.

📝 View the quick-reference notes sheet for this topic →

Sequence vs series — the key difference

A sequence is a list of terms: \(a_1, a_2, a_3, \ldots\). A series is what you get when you add those terms together: \(a_1+a_2+a_3+\cdots\). It's easy to mix the two words up, but they describe different things — a list versus a running total of that list.

The sum of the first \(n\) terms of a sequence is called a partial sum, usually written \(S_n\): \(S_n = a_1+a_2+\cdots+a_n\).

Sigma notation: a shorthand for sums

Writing out long sums term by term gets tedious, so mathematicians use the Greek letter \(\Sigma\) (capital sigma) as shorthand:

\[\sum_{k=1}^{n} a_k = a_1+a_2+\cdots+a_n\]

Read this as: "sum up \(a_k\), starting at \(k=1\), ending at \(k=n\)." The letter under the \(\Sigma\) (here \(k\)) is just a counting variable — it doesn't matter what letter you use, as long as you're consistent.
Worked example
\[\sum_{k=1}^{4} k^2 = 1^2+2^2+3^2+4^2 = 1+4+9+16=30\]
Always double-check the starting and ending values under and above the \(\Sigma\) — a sum from \(k=1\) to \(4\) has 4 terms, but a sum from \(k=3\) to \(4\) only has 2 (the terms for \(k=3\) and \(k=4\)). It's a common mistake to assume the number of terms equals the top limit.

Sum of an arithmetic series

There's a direct formula for the sum of the first \(n\) terms of an arithmetic sequence, without needing to add them one by one:

\[S_n = \frac{n}{2}\big(2a+(n-1)d\big)\]

Worked example
Sum the first 10 terms of the arithmetic sequence with \(a=5\), \(d=3\): \[S_{10} = \frac{10}{2}\big(2(5)+(9)(3)\big) = 5(10+27) = 5(37)=185\]

Sum of a geometric series

Similarly, geometric series have their own direct formula (for \(r\neq1\)):

\[S_n = \frac{a(1-r^n)}{1-r}\]

Worked example
Sum the first 5 terms of the geometric sequence with \(a=3\), \(r=2\): \[S_5 = \frac{3(1-2^5)}{1-2} = \frac{3(1-32)}{-1} = \frac{3(-31)}{-1} = 93\]
Try it: Evaluate \(\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{5}(2k+1)\) by expanding it term by term.
Show answer
\[(2(1)+1)+(2(2)+1)+(2(3)+1)+(2(4)+1)+(2(5)+1) = 3+5+7+9+11 = 35\]
Try it: Find the sum of the first 6 terms of the arithmetic sequence with \(a=2\), \(d=5\), using the formula.
Show answer
\[S_6 = \frac{6}{2}\big(2(2)+(5)(5)\big) = 3(4+25)=3(29)=87\]
