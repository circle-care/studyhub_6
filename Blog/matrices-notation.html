What is a matrix, and how do we add them?

Starting from scratch: rows, columns, dimensions, and the basic operations.

📝 View the quick-reference notes sheet for this topic →

What a matrix actually is

A matrix is just a rectangular grid of numbers, arranged in rows and columns. For example:

\[A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 5 & 1 \\ 0 & -3 & 4 \end{pmatrix}\]

This matrix has 2 rows and 3 columns, so we say it has dimensions \(2 \times 3\) (always rows first, then columns — easy to remember as "Rows by Columns", like reading a book).

Each individual number inside a matrix is called an entry or element. The entry in row \(i\), column \(j\) is usually written \(a_{ij}\). So in the matrix above, \(a_{12}=5\) (row 1, column 2).

Matrices are useful because they let you store and manipulate a whole grid of related numbers — like a system of equations, a transformation, or a network — as a single object, with its own rules for arithmetic.

Adding and subtracting matrices

This part is simple: add matching entries, position by position. The catch is that both matrices must have exactly the same dimensions — you can't add a \(2\times3\) matrix to a \(3\times2\) matrix, even though they contain the same number of entries.

Worked example
\[\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 3 & 4 \end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix} 5 & -1 \\ 0 & 2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1+5 & 2+(-1) \\ 3+0 & 4+2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 6 & 1 \\ 3 & 6 \end{pmatrix}\]
Try it: Compute \(\begin{pmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ -1 & 3 \end{pmatrix} - \begin{pmatrix} 4 & 1 \\ 2 & -2 \end{pmatrix}\).
Show answer
\[\begin{pmatrix} 2-4 & 0-1 \\ -1-2 & 3-(-2) \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} -2 & -1 \\ -3 & 5 \end{pmatrix}\]

Scalar multiplication

Multiplying a matrix by an ordinary number (called a scalar in this context) means multiplying every single entry by that number.

Worked example
\[3\begin{pmatrix} 1 & -2 \\ 0 & 4 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & -6 \\ 0 & 12 \end{pmatrix}\]
Don't confuse this with matrix multiplication (multiplying two matrices together) — that's a completely different, more involved process, covered in the next post. Scalar multiplication is just "everybody gets multiplied by the same single number."

The zero matrix and the identity matrix

Two special matrices come up constantly:

The zero matrix has every entry equal to 0 — it behaves like the number 0 does in ordinary arithmetic (adding it changes nothing).

The identity matrix \(I\) has 1s down the main diagonal and 0s everywhere else, e.g. \(I = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}\) for a \(2\times2\) case. It behaves like the number 1 (multiplying by it, once you know how matrix multiplication works, changes nothing).
Try it: What are the dimensions of \(\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & -2 \end{pmatrix}\)? Is this a row or column arrangement?
Show answer
Dimensions are \(1\times3\) (1 row, 3 columns) — this is called a row vector. A matrix with only one column instead (like \(\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\-2\end{pmatrix}\)) would be a column vector, with dimensions \(3\times1\).
