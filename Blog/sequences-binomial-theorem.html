Part 4 of 6: The binomial theorem

Sequences · Part 4

Expanding brackets without the pain

A formula for expanding \((a+b)^n\) directly, without multiplying out bracket after bracket.

📝 View the quick-reference notes sheet for this topic →

The problem this solves

Expanding \((a+b)^2\) by hand is easy: \(a^2+2ab+b^2\). But \((a+b)^7\) by repeated expansion would take forever. The binomial theorem gives a direct formula for any power \(n\), using coefficients from Pascal's triangle.

The formula

\[(a+b)^n = \sum_{k=0}^{n} \binom{n}{k} a^{n-k}b^k\]

Written out, this means: the power of \(a\) starts at \(n\) and decreases by 1 each term; the power of \(b\) starts at 0 and increases by 1 each term; the two powers always add up to \(n\). Each term is scaled by the binomial coefficient \(\binom{n}{k}\), read "n choose k".

The binomial coefficient itself is calculated as:

\[\binom{n}{k} = \frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}\]

where \(n!\) ("n factorial") means \(n\times(n-1)\times(n-2)\times\cdots\times1\).

Worked example: expanding (a+b)⁴

Worked example

The coefficients for \(n=4\) (row 4 of Pascal's triangle) are \(1,4,6,4,1\):

\[(a+b)^4 = a^4 + 4a^3b + 6a^2b^2 + 4ab^3 + b^4\]

Check the pattern: powers of \(a\) go \(4,3,2,1,0\); powers of \(b\) go \(0,1,2,3,4\) — each pair always sums to 4.

What changes with a negative b

If you're expanding something like \((x-2)^3\) (i.e. \(b=-2\), a negative number), be careful — every odd power of \(b\) will carry a negative sign through into the term.

Worked example
Expand \((x-2)^3\) using coefficients \(1,3,3,1\) (row 3), with \(a=x\), \(b=-2\): \[(x-2)^3 = x^3 + 3x^2(-2) + 3x(-2)^2 + (-2)^3\] \[= x^3 - 6x^2 + 12x - 8\]
A very common slip: forgetting to cube or square the whole \(-2\), including its sign. \((-2)^2=4\) (positive, since squaring a negative gives a positive), but \((-2)^3=-8\) (negative, since an odd power keeps the negative sign). Always compute powers of negative numbers carefully, term by term.

Try it yourself

Try it: What are the binomial coefficients for \(n=5\) (i.e. row 5 of Pascal's triangle)?
Show answer
\(1, 5, 10, 10, 5, 1\) — six coefficients in total, since \(n=5\) gives \(n+1=6\) terms.
Try it: Find the coefficient of \(x^2\) in the expansion of \((x+3)^4\).
Show answer
The \(x^2\) term comes from \(k=2\) (since the power of \(a=x\) is \(n-k=4-2=2\)): \[\binom{4}{2}x^2(3)^2 = 6\times x^2\times 9 = 54x^2\] So the coefficient is 54.
