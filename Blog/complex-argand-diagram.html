Part 2 of 6: The Argand Diagram

Complex Numbers · Part 2

Giving complex numbers a home: the Argand diagram

Why complex numbers can be drawn as points, and what that picture tells you.

📝 View the quick-reference notes sheet for this topic →

One number, two coordinates

A real number lives on a single line — the number line. But a complex number \(z=a+bi\) is built from two independent pieces of information, \(a\) and \(b\). Two independent numbers naturally suggest two dimensions, not one. That's the whole idea behind the Argand diagram (also just called the complex plane): plot \(z=a+bi\) as the ordinary point \((a,b)\) on a 2D grid.

The horizontal axis is the real axis (values of \(a\)). The vertical axis is the imaginary axis (values of \(b\)). Every complex number corresponds to exactly one point, and every point corresponds to exactly one complex number.

Adding complex numbers geometrically

Here's where the picture starts paying for itself. If you plot \(z_1=a+bi\) and \(z_2=c+di\), their sum \(z_1+z_2=(a+c)+(b+d)i\) can be found geometrically using exactly the same parallelogram rule you'd use to add two vectors: draw both points from the origin, complete the parallelogram they form, and the fourth corner is the sum.

Try it: Plot \(2+i\) and \(1+3i\) mentally (or on paper) and use the parallelogram rule to find their sum. Then check it algebraically.
Show answer
Algebraically: \((2+i)+(1+3i) = 3+4i\). The parallelogram's fourth corner should land exactly on the point \((3,4)\) — matching the algebra.

Conjugation as a reflection

Recall the conjugate \(\bar z = a-bi\) just flips the sign of the imaginary part. On the Argand diagram, that's precisely a reflection in the real axis — the point \((a,b)\) flips to \((a,-b)\), exactly like folding the page along the horizontal axis.

Worked example
\(z = 5+4i\) sits above the real axis. Its conjugate \(\bar z = 5-4i\) sits the same distance below it, directly mirrored.
Negation, \(z \to -z\), is a different transformation: it's a \(180°\) rotation about the origin (reflecting through the origin, not just the axis), because both the sign of \(a\) and the sign of \(b\) flip.

Why the picture matters

The Argand diagram isn't just decoration — it becomes essential in the next few topics. Modulus and argument (distance and angle) only make intuitive sense once you're thinking of complex numbers as points. De Moivre's roots being "evenly spaced around a circle" is a picture, not just an equation. Keeping this geometric picture in your head from the start makes everything that follows click into place faster.

Try it: Without drawing, describe where \(-3-2i\) would sit relative to the origin (which quadrant?).
Show answer
Both the real part (\(-3\)) and imaginary part (\(-2\)) are negative, so the point sits in the third quadrant — left of and below the origin.
