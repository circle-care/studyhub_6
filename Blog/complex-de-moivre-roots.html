Part 5 of 6: De Moivre's formula & roots

Complex Numbers · Part 5

Powers made easy, and the secret to finding roots

One formula that turns painful repeated multiplication into simple arithmetic on angles.

📝 View the quick-reference notes sheet for this topic →

Raising a complex number to a power

Imagine trying to compute \((1+i)^{10}\) by expanding brackets directly — ten repeated multiplications, each one messier than the last. Exponential form rescues you from this entirely, because raising \(re^{i\theta}\) to the power \(n\) is just ordinary index laws:

\[z^n = \left(re^{i\theta}\right)^n = r^n e^{in\theta}\]

Converting back to polar form (cos/sin) gives what's known as De Moivre's formula:

\[z^n = r^n(\cos n\theta + i\sin n\theta)\]

In words: raise the modulus to the power \(n\), and simply multiply the angle by \(n\). That's the entire calculation — no repeated expansion needed.

Worked example
Compute \((1+i)^4\).

First convert to exponential form (as in Part 4): \(1+i = \sqrt2\,e^{i\pi/4}\).

Apply De Moivre's formula with \(n=4\):

\[(1+i)^4 = (\sqrt2)^4\,e^{i\cdot4\cdot(\pi/4)} = 4\,e^{i\pi}\]

Converting back: \(e^{i\pi}=\cos\pi+i\sin\pi=-1+0i=-1\), so \((1+i)^4 = 4(-1) = -4\).

Turning the formula around: finding roots

If De Moivre's formula tells you how to raise a number to a power, running it backwards tells you how to find roots. Suppose you want to solve \(z^n=w\) for \(z\), where \(w=s(\cos\phi+i\sin\phi)\) is already known. The formula for all \(n\) solutions is:

\[z_k = s^{1/n}\left(\cos\frac{\phi+2\pi k}{n}+i\sin\frac{\phi+2\pi k}{n}\right), \qquad k=0,1,2,\ldots,n-1\]

The \(+2\pi k\) term is the crucial part, and it's easy to forget. Since angles repeat every \(2\pi\), \(w\) can equally be described using the angle \(\phi\), or \(\phi+2\pi\), or \(\phi+4\pi\), and so on — and each of these, divided by \(n\), gives a genuinely different angle before the division "wraps back around". That's why an \(n\)th root equation has exactly \(n\) distinct solutions, not just one.

Roots of unity: the cleanest example

The simplest possible case is solving \(z^n=1\). Since \(1 = 1(\cos0+i\sin0)\), we have \(s=1\), \(\phi=0\), so the formula simplifies to:

\[z_k = \cos\frac{2\pi k}{n}+i\sin\frac{2\pi k}{n}\]

All \(n\) of these solutions have modulus 1 (they all lie exactly on the unit circle), and they're evenly spaced around it by an angle of \(\frac{2\pi}{n}\) each.

Worked example
Solve \(z^3=1\).

With \(n=3\), \(s=1\), \(\phi=0\):

\[z_k = \cos\frac{2\pi k}{3}+i\sin\frac{2\pi k}{3}, \quad k=0,1,2\]

giving \(z_0=1\), \(z_1=-\frac12+\frac{\sqrt3}{2}i\), and \(z_2=-\frac12-\frac{\sqrt3}{2}i\) — three points equally spaced by \(120°\) around the unit circle.

Always convert to polar/exponential form before applying De Moivre's formula or the root formula — neither works directly on Cartesian form.

Try it yourself

Try it: Use De Moivre's formula to compute \((\sqrt3+i)^3\). (First find modulus and argument.)
Show answer
\(r=\sqrt{3+1}=2\), \(\theta = \arctan(1/\sqrt3) = \pi/6\) (1st quadrant). So: \[(\sqrt3+i)^3 = 2^3\left(\cos\frac{3\pi}{6}+i\sin\frac{3\pi}{6}\right)=8\left(\cos\frac{\pi}{2}+i\sin\frac{\pi}{2}\right)=8(0+i)=8i\]
Try it: How many distinct solutions does \(z^5=32\) have, and what's their modulus?
Show answer
There are exactly \(5\) distinct roots (since \(n=5\)), and each has modulus \(s^{1/n} = 32^{1/5}=2\) — all five roots lie on a circle of radius 2, spaced \(72°\) apart.
