Turning recurring decimals into fractions

A surprising application of geometric series: recurring decimals are secretly infinite geometric sums.

📝 View the quick-reference notes sheet for this topic →

Why a recurring decimal is a geometric series in disguise

Take \(0.777\ldots\) (the 7 repeating forever). This is secretly the sum:

\[0.7+0.07+0.007+\cdots\]

Each term is \(\frac{1}{10}\) of the one before it — that's a geometric series with \(a=0.7\) and \(r=0.1\). Since \(|r|<1\), this infinite series actually converges to a finite value (this is the "sum to infinity" idea), given by:

\[S_\infty = \frac{a}{1-r}\]

Method 1: the geometric series formula

Worked example
Convert \(0.777\ldots\) to a fraction using \(a=0.7\), \(r=0.1\): \[S_\infty = \frac{0.7}{1-0.1} = \frac{0.7}{0.9} = \frac{7}{9}\] Check: \(7\div9 = 0.777\ldots\) ✓

Method 2: algebraic subtraction

There's a second, purely algebraic method that doesn't require recognising the geometric series at all — useful as a cross-check, or if the series formula slips your mind under exam pressure.

Worked example

Let \(x=0.777\ldots\). Multiply both sides by 10 (shifting the decimal point past one repeating block):

\[10x = 7.777\ldots\]

Subtract the original equation from this one — the repeating parts cancel exactly:

\[10x-x = 7.777\ldots-0.777\ldots\] \[9x = 7\] \[x = \frac{7}{9}\]
Both methods agree, as they should — pick whichever feels more natural to you. The algebraic method generalises well to longer repeating blocks (e.g. multiply by 100 if two digits repeat, by 1000 if three digits repeat, and so on).

A longer repeating block

Worked example
Convert \(0.\overline{45}\) (i.e. \(0.454545\ldots\), with "45" repeating) using the algebraic method:

Since two digits repeat, multiply by 100:

\[x=0.454545\ldots \qquad 100x=45.454545\ldots\] \[100x-x = 45.454545\ldots - 0.454545\ldots\] \[99x=45 \quad\Rightarrow\quad x=\frac{45}{99}=\frac{5}{11}\]
Always simplify your final fraction fully — \(\frac{45}{99}\) reduces to \(\frac{5}{11}\) by dividing top and bottom by their common factor of 9. Exam mark schemes generally expect the fraction in lowest terms.
Try it: Convert \(0.333\ldots\) to a fraction using the geometric series method (\(a=0.3\), \(r=0.1\)).
Show answer
\[S_\infty = \frac{0.3}{1-0.1} = \frac{0.3}{0.9} = \frac{1}{3}\]
Try it: Convert \(0.\overline{18}\) (i.e. \(0.181818\ldots\)) to a fraction using the algebraic method.
Show answer
\(x=0.181818\ldots\), multiply by 100 (two digits repeat): \[100x=18.181818\ldots\] \[100x-x=18 \quad\Rightarrow\quad 99x=18 \quad\Rightarrow\quad x=\frac{18}{99}=\frac{2}{11}\]
