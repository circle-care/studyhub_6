Part 4 of 6: Polar & exponential form

Complex Numbers · Part 4

Rewriting complex numbers using distance and angle

Why polar and exponential form exist, and why they make multiplication so much easier.

📝 View the quick-reference notes sheet for this topic →

From (a, b) to (r, θ)

So far you've described a complex number two different ways: Cartesian form \(z=a+bi\) (real and imaginary parts), and separately, modulus \(r=|z|\) and argument \(\theta=\arg(z)\) (distance and angle). It turns out you can rebuild the Cartesian form directly from \(r\) and \(\theta\), using basic trigonometry:

\[a = r\cos\theta, \qquad b = r\sin\theta\]

Substituting these into \(z=a+bi\) gives the polar form:

\[z = r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)\]

This is exactly the same number as \(a+bi\) — just written in terms of distance and direction instead of horizontal and vertical components.

Worked example
Convert \(z=1+i\) into polar form.

First find \(r\) and \(\theta\):

\[r = \sqrt{1^2+1^2}=\sqrt2, \qquad \theta = \arctan(1) = \frac{\pi}{4} \text{ (1st quadrant, so no adjustment needed)}\]

So:

\[z = \sqrt2\left(\cos\frac{\pi}{4}+i\sin\frac{\pi}{4}\right)\]

Euler's formula: an even shorter way to write it

One of the most remarkable results in all of mathematics is Euler's formula:

\[e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta\]

This says the (seemingly unrelated) exponential function, imaginary numbers, and trigonometry are all secretly connected. You don't need to derive this here — just accept it as a definition/identity you can use — but it lets you rewrite polar form even more compactly as exponential form:

\[z = re^{i\theta}\]

So the same number \(1+i\) from above can also be written as \(\sqrt2\,e^{i\pi/4}\).

Why bother? Multiplication becomes addition

Here's the payoff. Multiplying two complex numbers in Cartesian form means expanding brackets. In exponential form, the ordinary rules of exponents apply — multiplying just means adding the angles and multiplying the moduli:

\[z_1 z_2 = r_1e^{i\theta_1}\cdot r_2e^{i\theta_2} = r_1r_2\,e^{i(\theta_1+\theta_2)}\]

Written back in polar form, this is:

\[zw = rs\big(\cos(\theta+\phi)+i\sin(\theta+\phi)\big)\]

And division works the same way, but subtracting angles:

\[\frac{z}{w} = \frac{r}{s}\big(\cos(\theta-\phi)+i\sin(\theta-\phi)\big)\]

Worked example
Multiply \(z=2e^{i\pi/6}\) and \(w=3e^{i\pi/3}\): \[zw = (2)(3)\,e^{i(\pi/6+\pi/3)} = 6e^{i\pi/2}\]

Converting back: \(e^{i\pi/2} = \cos\frac{\pi}{2}+i\sin\frac{\pi}{2} = 0+i(1) = i\), so \(zw = 6i\).

Watch your sign for \(r\): the modulus \(r\) must always come out non-negative. If a calculation ever gives you a negative "\(r\)", it means you should instead add \(\pi\) to \(\theta\) and make \(r\) positive — never leave \(r\) negative in a final polar/exponential answer.

Bonus: Euler's formula gives you trig identities for free

Since \(e^{i\theta}=\cos\theta+i\sin\theta\) and \(e^{-i\theta}=\cos\theta - i\sin\theta\) (because cosine is even and sine is odd), adding or subtracting these two equations and rearranging gives:

\[\cos\theta = \frac{e^{i\theta}+e^{-i\theta}}{2}, \qquad \sin\theta = \frac{e^{i\theta}-e^{-i\theta}}{2i}\]

These identities become genuinely useful once you study calculus involving trig functions, or when proving other trig identities.

Try it: Convert \(z=-2\) into exponential form. (Hint: where does \(-2\) sit on the real axis, and what angle is that?)
Show answer
\(-2\) sits on the negative real axis, distance 2 from the origin, at angle \(\pi\) (180°). So \(r=2\), \(\theta=\pi\), giving \(z = 2e^{i\pi}\). (This is actually the famous relation behind Euler's identity, covered in Part 6.)
Try it: Divide \(z=6e^{i2\pi/3}\) by \(w=2e^{i\pi/3}\).
Show answer
\[\frac{z}{w} = \frac{6}{2}e^{i(2\pi/3-\pi/3)} = 3e^{i\pi/3}\]
