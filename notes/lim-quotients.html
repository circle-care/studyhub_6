Limits of Quotients
Main points
\[ \lim_{x \to a} \frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = \frac{\lim_{x \to a} f(x)}{\lim_{x \to a} g(x)} \] provided \( \lim_{x \to a} g(x) \neq 0 \).

This is the quotient law we already know — as long as the bottom limit isn't zero, just find each limit separately and divide.

Example: \( \lim_{x \to 2} \dfrac{x+3}{x} \)

\( \lim_{x\to2}(x+3)=5 \), \( \lim_{x\to2} x = 2 \), denominator \( \neq 0 \)
\[ \lim_{x \to 2} \frac{x+3}{x} = \frac{5}{2} \]

Case: numerator nonzero, denominator → 0.

If \( f(a) \neq 0 \) and \( g(a) = 0 \), the limit does not exist. Dividing by an ever-smaller denominator produces an ever-larger (unbounded) result — it can't settle on one value.
Examples / explanation

Case: both numerator and denominator → 0 (the \( \frac{0}{0} \) case).

\( \dfrac{0}{0} \) is not straightforward. Just knowing both approach zero tells us nothing on its own — we need to know how they approach zero relative to each other. It could give a large number, a small number, or something in between. We don't give up — we do more work.
Simple check first: \( \dfrac{2x}{x} \)

We don't care what happens exactly at \( x=0 \) — everywhere else, \( \dfrac{2x}{x}=2 \). So \[ \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{2x}{x} = 2 \]

Example 1: \( \lim\limits_{x \to 0} \dfrac{x^2+2x-3}{x^2-3x+2} \)

Both numerator and denominator are polynomials — continuous everywhere. Denominator at \( x=0 \) is \( 2 \neq 0 \), so just substitute: \[ \frac{0+0-3}{0-0+2} = \frac{-3}{2} \]

Example 2: \( \lim\limits_{x \to 1} \dfrac{x^2+2x-3}{x^2-3x+2} \)

Substituting \( x=1 \) gives \( \dfrac{0}{0} \) — indeterminate. Factorise both: \[ \frac{x^2+2x-3}{x^2-3x+2} = \frac{(x+3)(x-1)}{(x-2)(x-1)} \] Cancel the common factor \( (x-1) \): \[ = \frac{x+3}{x-2} \] Now substitute \( x=1 \): \[ \frac{1+3}{1-2} = \frac{4}{-1} = -4 \]

Example 3: \( \lim\limits_{x \to -1} \dfrac{x-1}{x+\frac{1}{x}+2} \)

Substituting \( x=-1 \) directly is messy because of the \( \frac{1}{x} \) inside the denominator. Multiply numerator and denominator by \( x \) to clear it: \[ \frac{x-1}{x+\frac{1}{x}+2} \times \frac{x}{x} = \frac{x(x-1)}{x^2+2x+1} \] The denominator now factorises as a perfect square: \[ x^2+2x+1 = (x+1)^2 \] So the expression becomes: \[ \frac{x(x-1)}{(x+1)^2} \] Now substitute \( x=-1 \): numerator \( = (-1)(-2) = 2 \), denominator \( = (0)^2 = 0 \). \[ \frac{2}{0} \] Numerator is nonzero, denominator is \( 0 \) — this is the "numerator ≠ 0, denominator → 0" case from the Main points column. \[ \lim_{x \to -1} \frac{x-1}{x+\frac{1}{x}+2} = \text{DNE} \]
Helpful points
Always substitute first. If you get a real number → done.
If you get \( \frac{\text{nonzero}}{0} \) → limit DNE.
If you get \( \frac{0}{0} \) → more work needed (factorise, rationalise, etc.).
\( \frac{0}{0} \) is called an indeterminate form — it doesn't tell you the answer directly, only that you need to simplify before you can see the limit.
