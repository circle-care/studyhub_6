Reading combined limits (+, −, ×, ÷) straight off a graph
Main points

When \( f \) and \( g \) are both graphed, use the limit laws directly on the heights the curves approach:

\[ \lim_{x\to a}[f\pm g] = \lim_{x\to a}f \pm \lim_{x\to a}g \] \[ \lim_{x\to a}[fg] = \lim_{x\to a}f \cdot \lim_{x\to a}g \] \[ \lim_{x\to a}\frac{f}{g} = \frac{\lim_{x\to a}f}{\lim_{x\to a}g}, \quad \text{provided denominator} \neq 0 \]

The big rule about circles:

A limit only cares where the curve is heading — not what dot is actually plotted at that x-value. Open circles, filled circles, missing points: none of it changes the limit. It only matters if you're asked for \( f(a) \) itself, or whether the function is continuous there.

If either individual limit doesn't exist (e.g. a jump), the combined two-sided limit usually doesn't exist either — check one-sided limits separately in that case.

Examples / explanation

Example 1 — Addition, ignore the circles:

As \( x\to3 \): the curve for \( f \) heads to height 4 (open circle there, \( f(3)=1 \) plotted separately below). The curve for \( g \) heads to height 2 (filled circle, so \( g(3)=2 \) too). \[ \lim_{x\to3}[f(x)+g(x)] = 4 + 2 = 6 \] The open circle on \( f \) is irrelevant here — we only used where the curve was heading (4), never the plotted value \( f(3)=1 \).
\( f \) approaches 4 as \(x\to3\) (open circle) but is actually defined as 1 there (filled dot) — a removable discontinuity. The limit uses 4.

Example 2 — Division, denominator → 0 (nonzero numerator):

As \( x\to1 \): \( f(x) \to 4 \) (nonzero). \( g(x) \) crosses zero at \( x=1\): small and negative just left, small and positive just right (open circle exactly at zero, since \( g(1) \) is undefined). \[ \lim_{x\to1^-}\frac{f}{g} = \frac{4}{\text{small}-} = -\infty \qquad \lim_{x\to1^+}\frac{f}{g} = \frac{4}{\text{small}+} = +\infty \] One-sided limits disagree, so \( \lim_{x\to1}\dfrac{f}{g} \) does not exist.

Example 3 — Multiplication, one factor → 0:

As \( x\to0 \): \( f(x)\to0 \) (filled dot at the origin, \( f(0)=0 \) too — continuous there). \( g(x)\to-3 \). \[ \lim_{x\to0}[f(x)g(x)] = 0 \times(-3) = 0 \] Doesn't matter that \( g \) is a fairly large negative number — multiplying by something heading to 0 still gives 0.
Helpful points
When circle status is IGNORED:
Computing any limit — the curve's approach height is all that matters, regardless of open/filled/missing dots.
When circle status MATTERS:
• Evaluating \( f(a) \) itself (open circle → undefined; filled circle → that's the value)
• Deciding if \( f \) is continuous at \( a \) — needs limit and \( f(a) \) to exist and be equal
• Deciding if a discontinuity is removable (open circle where limit exists) vs a genuine jump/undefined point
Sign comboResult
\( \dfrac{+}{0^+} \)\( +\infty \)
\( \dfrac{+}{0^-} \)\( -\infty \)
\( \dfrac{0}{0} \)indeterminate — need more info

If one of \( f \) or \( g \) has no limit at \( a \) (e.g. a jump), the combined two-sided limit fails too — but the one-sided combined limits may still exist and be computable separately.
