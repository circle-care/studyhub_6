The binomial theorem
Main points

The binomial theorem quickly expands \((a+b)^n\) without multiplying it out term by term.

\[ (a+b)^n = \sum_{k=0}^{n} \binom{n}{k} a^{n-k}b^k \] The coefficients \(\binom{n}{k}\) (read "\(n\) choose \(k\)") are the binomial coefficients.

Two ways to find \(\binom{n}{k}\):

1. Pascal's Triangle: each number is the sum of the two above it.
2. Factorial formula (faster for large \(n\)): \[ \binom{n}{k} = \frac{n!}{k!(n-k)!} \]
Examples / explanation

Pascal's Triangle (rows 0–4):

Row 0: 1
Row 1: 1 1
Row 2: 1 2 1
Row 3: 1 3 3 1
Row 4: 1 4 6 4 1

Row \(n\) gives the coefficients for \((a+b)^n\). Row 4 → \(1,4,6,4,1\) are the coefficients for \((a+b)^4\).

Expanding using Pascal's Triangle:

\[ (a+b)^4 = 1a^4 + 4a^3b + 6a^2b^2 + 4ab^3 + 1b^4 \] Powers of \(a\) count down from 4 to 0; powers of \(b\) count up from 0 to 4, while coefficients come straight from row 4.

Using the factorial formula:

Find \(\binom{6}{2}\): \[ \binom{6}{2} = \frac{6!}{2!(6-2)!} = \frac{6!}{2!\,4!} = \frac{720}{2\times24} = \frac{720}{48} = 15 \]

Finding one specific term (no need to expand everything):

Find the term containing \(a^3b^2\) in \((a+b)^5\).

Here \(n=5\), and we want the term where \(b\) has power 2, so \(k=2\): \[ \binom{5}{2}a^{5-2}b^2 = \binom{5}{2}a^3b^2 = 10a^3b^2 \]

Coefficient of \(x^k\) in \((a+bx)^n\), with a negative \(b\):

Find the coefficient of \(x^3\) in \((2-5x)^6\).

Here \(a=2\), \(b=-5\) (the whole \(-5\), sign included), \(n=6\). Want \(x^3\), so \(k=3\): \[ \binom{6}{3}a^{6-3}b^3 = \binom{6}{3}(2)^3(-5)^3 \] \[ \binom{6}{3}=20, \qquad (-5)^3=-125 \quad\text{(odd power — stays negative)} \] \[ 20\times8\times(-125) = -20{,}000 \] Coefficient of \(x^3\) is \(-20{,}000\). Carry \(b=-5\) as one signed number the whole way through — don't split off the sign and reattach it at the end.
Helpful points
\(n! = n\times(n-1)\times(n-2)\times\cdots\times2\times1\). Special case: \(0! = 1\) (defined, not zero!) — needed so the formula works at the very ends of each row.
Pascal's Triangle is quick for small \(n\), but gets unwieldy fast for large \(n\) (e.g. \(n=12\)) — switch to the factorial formula once \(n\) gets much beyond 6 or so.
In \(\binom{n}{k}a^{n-k}b^k\), the two exponents \((n-k)\) and \(k\) always add up to \(n\) — a quick way to check you've picked the right \(k\) for a specific term.
Quick sign check when \(b\) is negative: if \(k\) is even, that term's coefficient is positive; if \(k\) is odd, it's negative. A fast sanity check before finishing the arithmetic.
