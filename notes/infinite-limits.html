In what way does the limit DNE? (Infinite Limits)
Main points

Saying a limit "does not exist" isn't always the full story. Sometimes it fails to exist because the function is blowing up — heading toward \( +\infty \) or \( -\infty \) — rather than jumping around unpredictably.

When \( f(x) \to \dfrac{\text{nonzero}}{0} \) as \( x \to a \), the function blows up near \( a \). We describe how it blows up using \( +\infty \) or \( -\infty \), separately for each side if needed.

Rule of thumb:

Work out the sign of the numerator and the sign of the (small) denominator separately on each side of \( a \). Numerator sign ÷ denominator sign tells you whether the function shoots up (\( +\infty \)) or down (\( -\infty \)).
\( +\infty \) and \( -\infty \) are not numbers — the limit still technically does not exist. Writing \( \lim = +\infty \) is a description of the behaviour, not a value.
Examples / explanation

Example 1: \( \displaystyle\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{1}{x} \)

As \( x \to 0^{+} \) (from the right), \( x \) is small and positive, so \( \dfrac{1}{x} \) is large and positive: \[ \lim_{x \to 0^{+}} \frac{1}{x} = +\infty \] As \( x \to 0^{-} \) (from the left), \( x \) is small and negative, so \( \dfrac{1}{x} \) is large and negative: \[ \lim_{x \to 0^{-}} \frac{1}{x} = -\infty \] The two one-sided limits disagree (in sign), so \( \displaystyle\lim_{x\to0}\frac1x \) does not exist — but now we know exactly how it fails.
\( y = \dfrac{1}{x} \) — shoots to \( +\infty \) on the right of 0, \( -\infty \) on the left. Open circle: undefined at \( x=0 \).

Example 2: \( \displaystyle\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{1}{x^{2}} \)

Here \( x^2 \) is positive whether \( x \) is positive or negative, so both sides behave the same way: \[ \lim_{x \to 0^{+}} \frac{1}{x^{2}} = +\infty \qquad \lim_{x \to 0^{-}} \frac{1}{x^{2}} = +\infty \] Both sides agree, so we can write \( \displaystyle\lim_{x\to0}\frac{1}{x^2} = +\infty \) — still DNE as a real number, but this time it's consistent on both sides.
\( y = \dfrac{1}{x^{2}} \) — both sides shoot up to \( +\infty \). Symmetric "U" shape either side of the open circle at \( x=0 \).

Example 3: \( \displaystyle\lim_{x \to 2} \frac{3x}{4-x^{2}} \)

Substitute \( x=2 \): numerator \( = 3(2) = 6 \). Denominator \( = 4 - 2^2 = 0 \). So we have \( \dfrac{6}{0} \) — nonzero over zero, meaning it blows up. Numerator is a constant sign (positive, \( =6 \)) near \( x=2 \), so everything depends on the sign of the denominator on each side.

Just left of 2 (\( x \) slightly less than 2, e.g. \( x=1.9 \)): \( 4-x^2 = 4-3.61 = 0.39 \), small and positive. \[ \frac{6}{\text{small }+} = \text{large }+ \;\Rightarrow\; \lim_{x\to2^{-}}\frac{3x}{4-x^2} = +\infty \] Just right of 2 (e.g. \( x=2.1 \)): \( 4-x^2 = 4-4.41 = -0.41 \), small and negative. \[ \frac{6}{\text{small }-} = \text{large }- \;\Rightarrow\; \lim_{x\to2^{+}}\frac{3x}{4-x^2} = -\infty \] One-sided limits disagree, so the two-sided limit does not exist — and now we know it's \( +\infty \) from the left, \( -\infty \) from the right.
\( y = \dfrac{3x}{4-x^{2}} \) near \( x=2 \) — rises to \( +\infty \) approaching from the left, drops from \( -\infty \) rising from the right.
Helpful points
Method for \( \dfrac{\text{nonzero}}{0} \) cases:
1. Confirm numerator \( \neq 0 \) at the point.
2. Check denominator sign just left and just right of the point (plug in a nearby value).
3. Combine signs: \( \dfrac{+}{+}=+\infty \), \( \dfrac{+}{-}=-\infty \), \( \dfrac{-}{+}=-\infty \), \( \dfrac{-}{-}=+\infty \).
If both one-sided infinite limits agree (same sign), you can write the two-sided limit as that same infinity. If they disagree, the two-sided limit simply does not exist — no infinity symbol covers both.
Don't confuse this with the \( \frac{0}{0} \) case from Page 4 — that one often simplifies to a real number. This is \( \frac{\text{nonzero}}{0} \), which never simplifies away; it always means blow-up.
