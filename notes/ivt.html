What is IVT?
Main points

The Intermediate Value Theorem (IVT) says a continuous function can't "skip" values — it has to pass through every value between two points.

If a function is continuous on an interval, its graph is one unbroken curve — so it can't jump from one output value to another without passing through everything in between.
IVT tells you a solution exists somewhere in the interval — it does not tell you exactly where, or how many solutions there are.

The sign change rule: a special case of IVT, used to hunt for roots.

If \( f \) is continuous on \( [a,b] \) and \( f(a) \) and \( f(b) \) have opposite signs (one positive, one negative), then \( 0 \) lies between them.

By IVT, there must be at least one \( c \) in \( (a,b) \) where \( f(c) = 0 \) — a root.

Why it works: \( 0 \) is just a specific value of \( M \). If the function goes from negative to positive (or positive to negative), it has to cross the x-axis somewhere on the way — it can't jump over zero without touching it, since it's continuous.

Same sign at both ends doesn't mean no root — it just means this test can't confirm one. There could still be roots, e.g. if the curve dips down and back up without crossing.
Examples / explanation
Intermediate Value Theorem:
If \( f \) is continuous on \( (a,b) \), right-continuous at \( a \), and left-continuous at \( b \), and \( M \) is between \( f(a) \) and \( f(b) \),
then there is at least one point \( c \) in \( (a,b) \) such that \[ f(c) = M \]

The graph below shows the idea: the curve runs continuously from \( f(a) \) up to \( f(b) \). Since \( M \) sits between those two values, the horizontal line \( y = M \) must cross the curve at least once — at \( x = c \).

The curve must cross \( y = M \) at least once between \( a \) and \( b \) — that crossing point is \( c \).

Worked example — root of a polynomial:

Let \( f(x) = x^4 - x - 1 \) and set \( f(x) = 0 \). Polynomials are continuous everywhere, so \( f \) is continuous on any interval we pick. We want to know where \( M = 0 \).

Try some values: \[ f(1) = 1 - 1 - 1 = -1 \] \[ f(2) = 16 - 2 - 1 = 13 \] Since \( f(1) < 0 \) and \( f(2) > 0 \), \( M = 0 \) lies between \( f(1) \) and \( f(2) \).

By IVT, there is at least one root \( c \) with \( 1 < c < 2 \). We can't say exactly where yet — just that a root definitely exists in \( (1,2) \).

Helpful points
IVT only applies if \( f \) is continuous on the interval. If there's a break or hole anywhere between \( a \) and \( b \), the theorem doesn't guarantee anything.
\( c \) is not necessarily unique — the curve could cross \( y = M \) more than once. IVT only guarantees at least one.

Common use: showing an equation has a root. If \( f(a) \) and \( f(b) \) have opposite signs, \( M = 0 \) lies between them, so there's a root \( c \) with \( f(c) = 0 \).

\( f \) only needs to be continuous on the closed interval \( [a,b] \). Outside that interval, \( f \) can be discontinuous — IVT doesn't care what happens beyond \( a \) or \( b \).
