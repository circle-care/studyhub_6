What are limits?
Main points

A function \( f(x) \) describes what happens when input values change.

\( f(x) \) maps inputs (values of \( x \)) to outputs (values of \( f(x) \)).

Why limits matter:

📐 Derivatives — use limits to find the slope of a tangent to a curve

∫ Integrals — use limits to find area under a curve (rectangles getting thinner — Riemann sum)

One-sided limits

Right-hand limit — approaching from the right:

\[ \lim_{x \to 2^+} f(x) = 2 \]

Left-hand limit — approaching from the left:

\[ \lim_{x \to 2^-} f(x) = -2 \]

Tends to infinity

If outputs grow rapidly and do not slow down, the limit tends to \( \infty \).

Worked examples and information

Using the function:

\[ f(x) = \frac{\sqrt{3 - 5x} + 2x^2 + 23}{x - 1} \]

As \( x \to 1 \) (getting closer from the left):

Input \( x \)Output \( f(x) \)
0.5−1.87
0.9−1.93
0.99−1.997
0.999−1.9997
→ 1→ −2

So \( f(x) \) is getting closer to −2 as \( x \) approaches 1.

Checking if a limit tends to \( \infty \):

Show input values increasing by a factor of 10. If the output also increases by a factor of 10 each time and does not slow down, the limit tends to \( \infty \).

1.84, 18.45, 184.2, 1999.5, 19998.45...

One-sided limits at x = 1

Helpful points
\( + \) superscript means approaching from the right
\( - \) superscript means approaching from the left

\( \lim_{x \to a^+} f(x) \) = right-hand limit

\( \lim_{x \to a^-} f(x) \) = left-hand limit

Important: A limit describes what the function is approaching — not necessarily what it equals at that point.
\( x \to a \) means \( x \) gets very close to \( a \) but does not have to equal \( a \).

If output keeps growing and does not slow down → tends to \( \infty \)

Behaviour and the overall limit
Main points

There can be an overall limit only if both the right-hand (RH) and left-hand (LH) limits are equal.

\[ \lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L \] when both LH and RH limits approach the same value \( L \).

The limit may not exist because:

① The function blows up (tends to \( \infty \))

② RH and LH limits exist but are not equal

Finding limits around a:

If function \( f(x) \) approaches the same value \( L \) as \( x \) approaches \( a \) from both sides, then the limit exists and equals \( L \).

\[ \lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L \]

Remember: \( x \) approaches \( a \), but \( x \neq a \). We never actually let \( x \) equal \( a \) when finding a limit.
Even if \( f(a) \) exists, that does not mean the limit equals \( f(a) \). These are separate questions.

Laws of limits — the first four are easy!
Main points

When you know two limits, you can work out the limit of combinations of those functions.

Example:
\( \lim_{x \to a} f(x) = 5 \) and \( \lim_{x \to a} g(x) = 3 \)

Then: \( \lim_{x \to a} [f(x) + g(x)] = 5 + 3 = 8 \)

The four laws work for one-sided limits too, as long as both sides are coming from the same direction.

These laws only work when the individual limits already exist. If a limit does not exist, you cannot apply the laws.
The four laws

If \( \lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L \) and \( \lim_{x \to a} g(x) = M \), then:

① Addition: \[ \lim_{x \to a} [f(x) + g(x)] = L + M \] ② Subtraction: \[ \lim_{x \to a} [f(x) - g(x)] = L - M \] ③ Multiplication: \[ \lim_{x \to a} [f(x) \times g(x)] = L \times M \] ④ Division (if \( M \neq 0 \)): \[ \lim_{x \to a} \frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = \frac{L}{M} \]
Worked example using law ③:
\( \lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L \), \( \lim_{x \to a} g(x) = M \)
\[ \lim_{x \to a} [f(x) \times g(x)] = L \times M \]
Helpful points
Division law — important condition:
Division only works if \( M \neq 0 \).
If \( M = 0 \), you cannot divide — the limit may not exist or may tend to \( \infty \).

Also holds for subtraction — works with one-sided limits as long as both are coming from the same direction.

You can combine these laws. For example, to find the limit of \( f(x) + g(x) - h(x) \), apply the addition law then the subtraction law.
Always check the individual limits exist before applying any law.

What is continuity?
Main points
Continuity: \( f(x) \) is continuous at \( x = a \) when: \[ \lim_{x \to a} f(x) = f(a) \]

But if \( \lim_{x \to a} f(x) \) does not equal \( f(a) \), we say it is discontinuous at that point.

Three types of discontinuity:

① Limit exists but \( f(a) \) does not exist (hole in graph — open circle)

② Limit exists but is not equal to \( f(a) \) (hole with the dot in the wrong place)

③ \( f(a) \) exists but the limit does not exist — because RH and LH limits are not equal

If \( \lim_{x \to a^-} f(x) = f(a) \) — function is right-continuous but not overall continuous.

Helpful points
Jump discontinuity:
RH and LH limits both exist and you can see them, but they are not equal so there is no overall limit — and therefore no continuity.
Removable discontinuity:
The overall limit exists (both sides agree), but the overall limit does not equal \( f(a) \). Called "removable" because you could fix it by redefining \( f(a) \).
Remember: Right-continuous at \( a \) does NOT mean overall continuous. You need the limit from both sides to equal \( f(a) \).

Overall Continuity
Main points
Continuity: \( f(x) \) is continuous at \( x = a \) when: \[ \lim_{x \to a} f(x) = f(a) \]

When is a function continuous everywhere?

A function is continuous everywhere — at every point — when you never have to lift your pen to draw it.

The limit of a continuous function is always the value of that function at that point.

Continuous functions (constants):

  • \( g(x) = x \)
  • \( |x| \)
  • \( \sin x \)
  • \( \cos x \)
NOT continuous: \( \tan x = \dfrac{\sin x}{\cos x} \) — always the exception, because \( \cos x = 0 \) at certain points, so \( \tan x \) blows up there.
