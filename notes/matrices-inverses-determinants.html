Determinants and inverses (2×2 matrices)
Main points
For \( A = \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{pmatrix} \): \[ \det A = ad - bc \]

The determinant is a single number. It tells you immediately whether \(A\) has an inverse.

\[ A^{-1} = \frac{1}{\det A}\begin{pmatrix} d & -b \\ -c & a \end{pmatrix} \] Swap \(a\) and \(d\), negate \(b\) and \(c\), then divide everything by the determinant.
\(A\) is invertible only if \(\det A \neq 0\). If \(\det A = 0\), \(A^{-1}\) does not exist — you'd be dividing by zero.
Examples / explanation

Computing a determinant:

\[ A = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 2 \\ 5 & 4 \end{pmatrix} \] \[ \det A = (3)(4) - (2)(5) = 12 - 10 = 2 \] Since \( \det A = 2 \neq 0\), \(A\) is invertible.

Finding the inverse:

Using the same \(A\), swap \(a,d\) and negate \(b,c\): \[ A^{-1} = \frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix} 4 & -2 \\ -5 & 3 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -2.5 & 1.5 \end{pmatrix} \]

Checking the answer:

A correct inverse must satisfy \(AA^{-1} = I\) (the identity matrix). Multiply them together and confirm you get \(\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}\) — a good habit to build in, and an easy way to catch arithmetic slips.

A singular (non-invertible) matrix:

\[ B = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 4 \\ 1 & 2 \end{pmatrix} \] \[ \det B = (2)(2)-(4)(1) = 4-4 = 0 \] \(B\) has no inverse — it's called a singular matrix. Notice row 2 is just row 1 halved — the rows aren't "independent," which is exactly what makes a matrix singular.
Helpful points
Memory trick for the inverse: "swap the main diagonal, negate the other diagonal, divide by the determinant."
Always calculate \(\det A\) first, before attempting the inverse. If it's 0, stop — there's no inverse, no matter what the question asks you to do with it.
Don't forget the \(\dfrac{1}{\det A}\) factor out the front — it's easy to swap/negate correctly but forget to divide through, which silently gives a wrong final matrix.
