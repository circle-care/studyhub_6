Networks, and solving linear equations with matrices
Main points

Networks: a matrix can represent connections/outputs between stages (e.g. factories → products, or routes between towns). Multiplying two such matrices gives the combined effect through both stages.

Linear equations in matrix form: \[ A\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b} \] where \(A\) holds the coefficients, \(\mathbf{x}\) is the column of unknowns, \(\mathbf{b}\) is the column of results.
Solving: if \(A^{-1}\) exists, \[ \mathbf{x} = A^{-1}\mathbf{b} \]
Examples / explanation

Network example:

Matrix \(P\) (2×2) gives units of raw material each of 2 factories needs per product; matrix \(Q\) (2×1) gives cost per unit of each raw material. Then \(PQ\) (2×1) gives the total material cost per factory — combining "factories → materials" with "materials → cost" into one "factories → cost" result via multiplication.

Writing simultaneous equations in matrix form:

\[ \begin{aligned} 2x + 3y &= 8 \\ x - y &= 1 \end{aligned} \] becomes \[ \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 3 \\ 1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 8 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} \] i.e. \(A\mathbf{x}=\mathbf{b}\) with \(A=\begin{pmatrix}2&3\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}\), \(\mathbf{b}=\begin{pmatrix}8\\1\end{pmatrix}\).

Solving using the inverse:

\[ \det A = (2)(-1)-(3)(1) = -5 \] \[ A^{-1} = \frac{1}{-5}\begin{pmatrix} -1 & -3 \\ -1 & 2 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0.2 & 0.6 \\ 0.2 & -0.4 \end{pmatrix} \] \[ \mathbf{x} = A^{-1}\mathbf{b} = \begin{pmatrix} 0.2 & 0.6 \\ 0.2 & -0.4 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 8 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1.6+0.6 \\ 1.6-0.4 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2.2 \\ 1.2 \end{pmatrix} \] So \(x = 2.2\), \(y = 1.2\). (Check: \(2(2.2)+3(1.2) = 4.4+3.6=8\) ✓, \(2.2-1.2=1\) ✓.)
Helpful points
This method only works if \(A^{-1}\) exists — if \(\det A = 0\), the system either has no solution or infinitely many, and this matrix method can't be used directly.
Order matters when solving: it's \(\mathbf{x}=A^{-1}\mathbf{b}\), NOT \(\mathbf{b}A^{-1}\) — matrix multiplication isn't commutative, so the inverse must multiply \(\mathbf{b}\) on the correct side.
Always double-check your final answer by substituting back into the original equations — a fast way to catch a sign slip in the inverse.

This is essentially the matrix version of solving \(ax=b\) by dividing: here "dividing by \(A\)" means multiplying by \(A^{-1}\), since matrices don't have division.
