Why do quadratics always have solutions once complex numbers exist?
Main points

Fundamental Theorem of Algebra: every degree-\(n\) polynomial equation (\(n\ge1\)) has at least one complex solution, and factorises into exactly \(n\) linear factors.

For a quadratic \(az^2+bz+c=0\) with real coefficients and negative discriminant, the roots are a complex conjugate pair.

Complex roots of any polynomial with real coefficients always occur in conjugate pairs.

Euler's identity: \(e^{i\pi} + 1 = 0\) — links \(0, 1, i, e, \pi\).

Examples / Explanation
Solve \(z^2 + 2z + 5 = 0\): \[z = \frac{-2 \pm \sqrt{4-20}}{2} = \frac{-2\pm\sqrt{-16}}{2} = -1 \pm 2i\]
Factorise \(z^3 - z^2 + 4z - 4\), given \(z=1\) is a root: Divide by \((z-1)\) to get \(z^2 + 4\), then \[z^2+4=0 \implies z = \pm 2i\] So \(z^3-z^2+4z-4 = (z-1)(z-2i)(z+2i)\).
Helpful points
Negative discriminant \(\Rightarrow\) complex conjugate roots — no real solutions exist, but complex ones always do.
Conjugate Root Theorem: if coefficients are real and \(p+qi\) is a root, then \(p-qi\) must also be a root.
Once you find one real root of a cubic, use polynomial (or synthetic) division to reduce it to a quadratic before solving further.
